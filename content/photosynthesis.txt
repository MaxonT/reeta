Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to turn light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. It is the main way energy from the sun enters almost every food chain on Earth.

The overall reaction
Photosynthesis takes in carbon dioxide and water and, using light energy, produces glucose and oxygen. The balanced equation is six carbon dioxide plus six water, in the presence of light, gives one glucose plus six oxygen. The oxygen is released as a byproduct, and most of the oxygen in the atmosphere comes from this process.

Where it happens
In plants, photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts, which are found mostly in the cells of leaves. Chloroplasts contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, which absorbs light mainly in the red and blue parts of the spectrum and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green. Inside the chloroplast, stacks of membranes called thylakoids hold the chlorophyll, and the fluid around them is called the stroma.

Stage one: the light-dependent reactions
The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, and that energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, hydrogen ions, and electrons. This splitting of water is where the oxygen released by plants comes from. The electrons pass along a chain of proteins, and their energy is used to make two energy-carrying molecules, ATP and NADPH. These reactions need light, which is why they are called light-dependent.

Stage two: the Calvin cycle
The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma and does not use light directly. It uses the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage to build sugar from carbon dioxide. An enzyme called rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon molecule, and a series of reactions then turns that carbon into a three-carbon sugar. Two of these three-carbon sugars combine to form glucose. The cycle also regenerates the starting molecule so it can keep running. Because it relies on the products of the light reactions, the Calvin cycle stops soon after light is removed.

How carbon dioxide gets in
Carbon dioxide enters a leaf through tiny pores called stomata, which are mostly on the underside. Each stoma is surrounded by two guard cells that open and close the pore. When stomata are open, carbon dioxide comes in but water vapor also escapes. On hot, dry days plants close their stomata to save water, which slows photosynthesis because less carbon dioxide can enter.

What affects the rate of photosynthesis
Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature all change how fast photosynthesis runs. As light intensity rises, the rate increases until another factor becomes limiting. Higher carbon dioxide levels also raise the rate up to a point. Temperature matters because enzymes such as rubisco work best in a certain range, and the rate drops sharply when it is too cold or when enzymes are damaged by too much heat. Whichever factor is in shortest supply is the limiting factor.

Why it matters
The glucose made in photosynthesis is used by the plant for energy through cellular respiration, and it is also converted into starch for storage and cellulose to build cell walls. Animals get this energy by eating plants or by eating other animals that ate plants. Photosynthesis also removes carbon dioxide from the air, which makes it important for the balance of gases in the atmosphere.
